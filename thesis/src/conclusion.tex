\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{chap:conclusion}

This thesis addressed a narrow but important gap in notebook reproducibility.
The FAIR Jupyter pipeline can identify a notebook that stopped because of a
missing or incompatible Python package, but it did not explain the failure or
try to repair it. As \Cref{chap:related-work} showed, failure detection,
dependency inference, and LLM-based repair already existed as separate ideas.
This work combines them in one bounded workflow that uses package evidence and
Docker re-execution rather than accepting an unverified language-model answer.

The resulting repair layer first classifies the failure and determines whether
it is within the pip-based repair scope. It then produces a structured,
plain-language explanation. For repair-eligible cases, the resolver verifies
package and import evidence before an action can reach Docker. The language
model may explain a failure or select from an already verified set of version
candidates, but it does not decide whether its own answer is valid. The
FixApplicator applies the selected action in the repository-specific Docker
environment and re-executes the complete notebook. A second repair round is
allowed only when the first one exposes a new, repair-eligible dependency
error. The result logger retains decisions, abstentions, evidence, and outcomes
for every attempt. The RML mapping and notebook-identity alignment were first
validated on pilot data and then locally materialised over the final live-PyPI
run. Publishing that validated artefact as a reusable repair graph remains
future work (\Cref{sec:v-i6,sec:fw-kg}).

The frozen implementation was covered by 711 passing automated tests. A later
collection inventory found 759 tests after further tests were added; this is a
coverage count, not a second passing-run result. The implementation was also
checked against live PyPI, an Ollama model service, Docker, Git repositories,
and the Morph-KGC mapping engine. The main evaluation used the 187 reserved records,
with Gemma-2~9B, live PyPI metadata, repository-specific Docker execution, and
at most two repair rounds. The 13 development records were used for prompt
design and pilots, not for the reported evaluation.

The three research questions from \Cref{sec:intro-rqs} are answered below.

\section*{RQ1: Explaining residual failures}

The explainer completed 180 of the 187 explanations for original failures and
all 69 explanations for dependency errors exposed after a first repair. Thus,
249 of the 256 encountered errors received a structured explanation. The seven
missing original explanations were runtime failures, not malformed outputs.
Completion and schema validity show that an explanation was available in the
expected form; they do not show that a reader found it useful or correct.

The reader studies provide that separate evidence. In the initial study, 15
participants gave 540 ratings of frozen LLM explanations, and 88.9\% were
Agree or Strongly Agree. The explanations were generally judged understandable,
useful, and plausible. A separate blinded comparison is more cautionary:
resolver-informed templates received higher descriptive ratings than the LLM
explanations on all six items for four selected errors. Both studies use small
convenience samples and measure perceived quality rather than objective
correctness. Within those limits, the answer to RQ1 is positive: the open-model
component produced structured explanations for most encountered failures, and
the participating readers generally found the selected explanations helpful.

\section*{RQ2: Resolving the targeted error and recovering the notebook}

The two parts of RQ2 must remain separate. In the live-PyPI evaluation, the
workflow recovered 5 of 187 notebooks completely (2.7\%). It made 204 repair
decisions that reached Docker: 24 ended in a Docker application error, and 180
were re-executed. Of those re-executed attempts, 147 removed the error they
targeted (81.7\%). A targeted error can disappear while a later dependency or
another error still prevents the notebook from completing. The 147/180 and
5/187 figures therefore answer different questions and should not be combined
into one success rate.

The pipeline also abstained on 52 of the 187 original failures because it
could not establish a sufficiently verified repair. This is a deliberate safety
result: the workflow does not install a distribution simply because its name
looks plausible. The remaining gap between targeted resolution and full
recovery reflects newly exposed errors, Docker-stage failures, and the
two-round bound. The worked NumPy case in \Cref{sec:v-case-study} demonstrates
that complete recovery is possible, but the aggregate result shows that package
repair alone cannot restore every notebook.

A separate matched comparison with a notebook-adapted PLLM-style baseline
provides additional context, not a replacement for the live-PyPI result. Under
the shared frozen package snapshot, the baseline fully recovered 6 notebooks
and the thesis pipeline recovered 3; the thesis pipeline had the higher
attempt-level targeted-error resolution rate (63.2\% versus 53.1\%). The
methods make different coverage and validation choices, so this single
descriptive comparison does not identify an overall winner. The answer to RQ2
is therefore qualified: evidence-grounded repair often removes the specific
error it addresses and sometimes restores complete execution, but it does not
guarantee reproducibility within two rounds.

\section*{RQ3: Sensitivity to the choice of model}

The frozen-metadata sensitivity evaluation ran the same pipeline with
Gemma-2~9B and Qwen3.6-35B-A3B-MLX-8bit. Qwen returned valid structured
explanations for 261 of 262 encountered errors; Gemma returned them for 238 of
264. Both configurations fully recovered three notebooks. Their targeted-error
resolution rates were also close: 92/150 (61.3\%) for Qwen and 96/152 (63.2\%)
for Gemma.

This is not an isolated model comparison. The provider and serving environment
differed, and two Docker outcomes differed despite identical repair decisions.
The small difference in repair rate therefore cannot be attributed to model
choice alone. RQ3 has a narrow answer: both grounded configurations operated
successfully under the frozen setup, while Qwen completed structured
explanations more reliably. The evidence does not show that either model is
generally superior.

\section*{Closing remark}

Taken together, the findings provide limited evidence that the approach is
feasible, rather than a general affirmative answer to the question that
motivated this thesis. In the evaluated setting, readers generally found the
selected explanations understandable, and evidence-grounded package actions
often removed the immediate error they targeted. Complete notebook recovery,
however, was rare. The key safeguard is that each proposed repair is checked
against package evidence and then against Docker re-execution; a fluent model
response alone is never accepted as proof of a repair.

The limits are equally important. The evaluation covers one reserved split of
third-party Python dependency failures, uses a two-round repair bound, and was
run once per configuration. It does not cover system dependencies, local files,
or general notebook errors. The reader studies are descriptive, and targeted
resolution is not complete recovery. \Cref{chap:future-work} sets out the next
steps: publish the locally materialised provenance graph for the final run,
extend coverage without weakening evidence checks, repeat the evaluation under
controlled conditions, and broaden the reader study. These steps would turn a
promising bounded repair workflow into a stronger and more reusable
reproducibility service.
